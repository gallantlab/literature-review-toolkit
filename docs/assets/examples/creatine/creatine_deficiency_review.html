Three disorders, one missing molecule: creatine deficiency of the brain, from discovery by spectroscopy to the search for a therapy

Creatine transporter deficiency and the two creatine synthesis defects, GAMT and AGAT deficiency, all empty the brain of creatine. Two of them can be treated with a dietary supplement, and the third has resisted every therapy tried so far. Abstract

The cerebral creatine deficiency syndromes are three inborn errors that share one deficit: too little creatine in the brain. GAMT and AGAT deficiency block the two steps of creatine synthesis, and creatine transporter deficiency (CTD), caused by variants in the X-linked gene SLC6A8, blocks creatine uptake into cells. All three cause intellectual disability, severe speech delay, epilepsy and behavioral problems. This review follows the field from its biochemical roots to the present, using a verified bibliography read through abstract-derived summaries. Creatine biochemistry had been worked out long before the disorders were found. They were found because in vivo proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) can see the creatine peak, and in each disorder that peak was missing. GAMT deficiency was described in 1994 and the other two in 2001. Biochemical tests in urine and blood then allowed patient screening, and GAMT deficiency has now entered newborn screening. Treatment has diverged sharply. Oral creatine restores brain creatine in both synthesis defects, and children treated before symptoms appear can develop normally. In CTD, oral creatine cannot enter the brain, and precursor supplements help a minority at best. The current candidates are creatine analogs and prodrugs, pharmacological correctors of misfolded transporters, and gene therapy, none yet proven in patients. MRS remains the defining diagnostic sign and the main biomarker of whether a treatment reaches the brain.

Section 1

Three disorders, one deficit

Creatine and phosphocreatine form an energy reserve in tissues whose demand for ATP rises and falls quickly, such as muscle and brain. Creatine is made in two steps. AGAT (L-arginine:glycine amidinotransferase, gene GATM) transfers the amidino group of arginine to glycine to make guanidinoacetate (GAA). GAMT (guanidinoacetate methyltransferase) then methylates GAA to creatine. Cells take creatine up through a sodium- and chloride-dependent transporter, SLC6A8. A defect at any of these three points lowers brain creatine.

The three disorders differ in what accumulates and in how they respond to treatment. In GAMT deficiency, GAA builds up while creatine falls. In AGAT deficiency, both GAA and creatine are low. In CTD, creatine is high in blood and urine but cannot reach the brain 1,2. The common clinical picture is intellectual disability, severe impairment of expressive speech, epilepsy and autistic behavior, with a movement disorder in GAMT deficiency 3,4,5,6 and a myopathy in AGAT deficiency 7,8. These disorders matter beyond their numbers because two of them are treatable, and they are counted among the inborn errors in which a diagnosis changes outcome 9,10.

This review covers five questions. How was creatine biology understood before the disorders were known? How were they discovered? How are patients found, by biochemistry and by spectroscopy? Why does losing creatine harm the brain? And how has treatment developed, from creatine supplementation to the therapies now in development for CTD? Section 2

Before the disorders: a century of creatine biochemistry

The chemistry needed to measure creatine is older than the field by a century. In 1886 Jaffe described the color reaction of creatinine with alkaline picrate 11, which was still being refined for creatinine measurement decades later 12. Later methods measured creatine directly with the diacetyl and α-naphthol reaction 13 and determined GAA among other guanidino compounds in biological fluids, later by chromatography 14,15. These assays later carried a trap. In GAMT deficiency, plasma creatinine measured by the Jaffe method can look normal when enzymatic or chromatographic methods show it is low 16.

Creatine's role in energy metabolism was established in muscle. In 1927 two groups found a labile phosphate compound in muscle, which Eggleton and Eggleton called phosphagen 17,18,19. It was identified as phosphocreatine 20. Creatine kinase, the enzyme that transfers phosphate between phosphocreatine and ADP, was crystallized in the 1950s 21. Brain and muscle were later found to carry distinct isoforms of the enzyme 22, and mitochondria carry their own 23,24.

The pathway of creatine synthesis was worked out in the 1940s and 1950s. Liver slices convert GAA (then called glycocyamine) into creatine 25,26, methionine was identified as the methyl donor 26, and glycine and arginine as precursors 27. Isotopes were used to measure the formation and loss of body creatine 28. The enzymatic mechanism of the methylation step was studied in 1954 29, and the transamidinase that forms GAA was purified 30. Creatine synthesis consumes a large share of the body's labile methyl groups and of arginine's amidino groups 31. Dietary creatine represses the transamidinase 32,33, and later work showed that this repression acts before translation 34. This feedback loop later became a lever for treating GAMT deficiency. Whether the brain makes its own creatine was asked as early as 1961 35. Creatine enters muscle by a saturable process 36, and the Walker review of 1979 consolidated the biosynthesis and its control 37.

The transporter itself was cloned in 1993. A cDNA from rabbit brain encoded a sodium- and chloride-dependent creatine transporter of the neurotransmitter transporter family 38. Human cDNAs followed 39,40. The gene was first mapped in mouse to a region conserved with human Xq28 40, and the human gene, SLC6A8, was assigned to Xq28 41,42. Human GAMT cDNA was cloned in 1995, prompted by the first patient suspected of a GAMT defect 43. A comprehensive review in 2000 summarized creatine and creatinine metabolism on the eve of the transporter and AGAT discoveries 44.

Two lines of theory explain why the brain should need creatine. The first is the phosphocreatine shuttle, the proposal that creatine kinase isoforms at sites of ATP production and use carry high-energy phosphate across the cell 24,45,46,47. Wallimann and colleagues extended this into the phosphocreatine circuit, for tissues with high and fluctuating energy demand 48. Energy budgets of the brain later showed that most of its energy is spent on signaling 49,50, which makes such a buffer plausible. Transmitting information is costly in ATP 51. Creatine also protects synaptic transmission during anoxia in brain slices 52. The second line treats creatine as a signaling molecule. Creatine is released from brain slices by an exocytotic, calcium-dependent process 53, and it has been found in synaptic vesicles 54. These ideas frame the disorders, but the corpus contains little direct evidence of which role matters most for the patients' symptoms.

Section 3

Seeing creatine: how in vivo spectroscopy made the disorders visible

The disorders were discovered with an instrument built for other purposes. In 1974 phosphorus NMR spectra were first recorded from intact tissue 55. Surface coils then allowed phosphorus spectra from the muscle and brain of living animals 56, and in 1983 phosphocreatine was measured in the brains of newborn infants 57. Chemical shift imaging, which maps spectra across space, was proposed in 1982 58.

Proton spectroscopy brought the creatine peak itself into view. Proton spectra of rat brain in vivo were obtained in 1983 59, and localized proton spectra of human brain at 1.5 T in 1985 60. Single-voxel localization methods, STEAM and PRESS, made proton spectra routine in human brain on clinical scanners 61,62,63. The resonance at 3.03 ppm reflects creatine and phosphocreatine together 64,65. Spectral fitting methods, above all LCModel, turned these spectra into metabolite concentrations 66,67.

One habit of the field deserves emphasis, because the disorders exposed it. Early quantitative studies often assumed a fixed total creatine of about 10 mM and expressed other metabolites as ratios to it 64,68. Absolute quantification studies measured creatine directly and found that it varies with region and age 69,70,71. Ratios to an assumed stable creatine can add more variability than they remove 72,73. In a creatine deficiency, a creatine-referenced ratio is not just noisy but misleading, because the reference itself is missing. Developmental norms for children, measured from the 1990s on, set the baseline against which a low creatine peak can be judged 74,70,75.

Section 4

Discovery: 1994 to 2001

GAMT deficiency was the first of the three to be found. In 1994 Stöckler and colleagues described a child with an extrapyramidal movement disorder and very low serum and urine creatinine. Proton MRS showed generalized depletion of brain creatine, and phosphorus MRS showed no phosphocreatine 76. Oral arginine raised brain GAA without raising creatine, whereas oral creatine raised brain creatine and lowered GAA. The authors presented it as a new and treatable inborn error. In 1996 the same group showed severe GAMT deficiency in liver and identified the mutant alleles 77, and reported that 25 months of creatine normalized brain creatine with substantial clinical improvement 78. Further patients followed with epilepsy and global delay 79,80. A milder presentation, with nonspecific intellectual disability and behavioral problems, was recognized in 2000 81.

CTD was found in 2001 from the opposite biochemical signature. Cecil and colleagues described a patient whose brain creatine was deficient on MRS while serum and urine creatine were high and GAA was normal. Oral creatine produced no measurable rise in brain creatine and no clinical improvement, and the authors proposed a defect of the creatine transporter 1. Salomons and colleagues reported an X-linked creatine deficiency syndrome in a boy with absent brain creatine, a hemizygous nonsense mutation in SLC6A8 and fibroblasts that could not take up creatine 2. Three heterozygous female relatives had mild biochemical abnormalities. Further families followed within two years 82,83,84.

AGAT deficiency was found the same year, after spectroscopy flagged two sisters. In 2000 Bianchi and colleagues described sisters with intellectual disability and severe language delay whose blood tests, metabolic screening and MRI were normal. Only proton MRS revealed generalized brain creatine depletion, which almost completely reversed with oral creatine 85. In 2001 Item and colleagues identified the cause as AGAT deficiency, with a homozygous nonsense mutation and low urinary GAA 86. A third patient was found in the same Italian pedigree 87. By 2003 the three disorders were reviewed as one group 3,4.

The order of discovery reflects the instrument. In all three disorders, the first sign that pointed to creatine was the absent or reduced creatine peak on brain MRS. The routine blood and urine tests of the time did not reveal them.

Section 5

Delineating the disorders

Creatine transporter deficiency. CTD is by far the most studied of the three in this corpus. Early descriptions established the core phenotype of developmental delay, severe expressive language impairment and mild epilepsy, with absent brain creatine 82,83,88 but preserved muscle creatine 88,89. Autistic behavior was listed among the hallmarks by 2003 90 and confirmed in further patients 91. Later reports added severe epilepsy 92, progressive intestinal and psychiatric problems in adult males 93 and cardiac findings 94. The largest retrospective series, 101 males from 85 families, found moderate to severe intellectual disability in most, with marked speech delay, behavioral problems, seizures, motor dysfunction and gastrointestinal problems 95. A third of these males had de novo mutations, and missense mutations with residual activity may give milder phenotypes.

Prospective natural history studies are recent. A study of 31 patients, compared with patients with fragile X syndrome, found symptoms before 24 months in most and a consistently delayed developmental trajectory 96. The Vigilan study followed 50 males aged 6 months to 65 years for up to four years 97. Most had significant intellectual disability, seizures, gastrointestinal symptoms and growth failure, all walked, and 78% developed some speech. Feeding problems combined with delayed sitting or crawling before the first birthday have been proposed as early signs that should prompt a urine screen 98. Cardiac findings remain unsettled. A cross-sectional study found a prolonged QTc in 39% of 18 males 99, whereas a later study of 23 males found prominent U-waves but no prolonged QTc once U-waves were excluded 100.

Females are the hardest group to diagnose. Heterozygous females in the first families already showed mild intellectual disability, learning problems or language impairments 2,82,88. A study of eight Dutch heterozygotes found raised urine creatine in only three, brain creatine that overlapped with controls, and skewed X-inactivation that made fibroblast uptake assays unreliable 101. A review of 32 reported female cases concluded that diagnosis in females requires molecular testing 102. A family survey found a mean age at diagnosis of 11.8 years in girls, after a mean age of 1.8 years at onset 103.

Interpreting SLC6A8 variants has its own literature. A fibroblast uptake assay classified missense variants as pathogenic or benign 104, and all known missense variants were classified functionally, with all variants deposited in a public database 105. Many pathogenic variants impair the processing and trafficking of the protein 106,107,108. The female carrier frequency was estimated at 0.024% from population exome data 109. In 2024 a ClinGen expert panel published gene-specific variant classification rules for GAMT, GATM and SLC6A8 110.

GAMT deficiency. A series of 27 patients found intellectual disability, epilepsy, GAA accumulation and brain creatine deficiency in all, with severe disability in 78% 111. Phenotype did not track genotype. A 2014 study of 48 individuals reported movement disorder in 13 and epilepsy in 35, and issued recommendations for diagnosis, treatment and monitoring 112. Epilepsy occurred in 81% of 58 reported cases and was drug-resistant in about 45% 113. A founder mutation, c.59G>C, accounts for most Portuguese alleles 114, and patients have been identified as adults in institutions for severe mental handicap 115. Estimates of frequency vary: a carrier frequency of 0.123% from exome data 116, an incidence near 1 in 250,000 from newborn sequencing 117, and a birth prevalence of 0.5 to 2 per million in the evidence review for newborn screening 118.

AGAT deficiency. AGAT deficiency is the rarest of the three. An international survey of 16 patients found intellectual or developmental disability in 15 and myopathy in 8 7. The myopathy can be prominent, presents in adulthood, and responds well to creatine 119,120,121,8. Febrile seizures had been noted in all three disorders 122, and focal epilepsy has been added to the AGAT phenotype recently 123,124. A separate, dominant disease of the same gene was found in 2018: heterozygous GATM missense variants cause renal Fanconi syndrome and kidney failure through aggregation of the enzyme in mitochondria 125.

Section 6

Finding patients: biochemical diagnosis and screening

Biochemical diagnosis rests on three metabolites: GAA, creatine and creatinine. GAA is high in GAMT deficiency and low in AGAT deficiency 126,4. The urine creatine-to-creatinine ratio is high in males with CTD 82,127. Low serum and urine creatinine was the first biochemical clue to GAMT deficiency 76,78. In 1997, low 24-hour urinary creatinine and raised urinary GAA, detected with the Sakaguchi reaction 128, were proposed as screening tools 129, and chromatography confirmed high GAA in all body fluids 130. Stable-isotope-dilution mass spectrometry made the measurement precise 131, and tandem mass spectrometry and HPLC methods made it fast and applicable to dried blood spots 132,133,134. A 2004 study established age-dependent reference values and called the raised urine creatine-to-creatinine ratio a pathognomonic marker of CTD 127. Enzyme assays in cultured cells confirm the synthesis defects 135,86,136,137, and a fibroblast uptake assay confirms CTD 2,82,104.

Each marker has known failure modes. A diet rich in beef or oily fish raises the urine creatine ratio and causes false positives 138. In a series of 41 patients, plasma GAA was fully specific for both synthesis defects but less sensitive for AGAT deficiency, and the urine ratio was fully specific in males with CTD but insensitive in females 139. Plasma can also reveal CTD, mainly through a markedly decreased plasma creatinine 140. Laboratory practice was standardized in a 2017 technical standard of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics 141.

Screening of high-risk groups set the prevalence estimates. Sequencing of a European panel of families with X-linked intellectual disability found SLC6A8 mutations in at least 2.1% 142, and sequencing of 478 males with unexplained intellectual disability suggested about 1% 143. Urine screening of 1,600 males found four confirmed cases 138, and screening of 6,353 French patients with unexplained neurological symptoms found a prevalence of 0.25% for all creatine deficiencies 144. Screening children with nonsyndromic autism found no cases among 443 145. Guidelines for the evaluation of developmental delay have moved accordingly. A 2003 practice parameter did not recommend routine metabolic screening 146, whereas later reviews placed creatine disorders among the treatable conditions worth testing for 147,9.

Newborn screening is the main change of the last decade, and it applies to GAMT deficiency. GAA was raised in the neonatal blood spot of a GAMT patient as early as 2001 133, and neonatal screening was judged feasible the same year 132. A neonate treated before symptoms developed normally 148, and archived blood spots of further cases confirmed the signal 149. Pilot studies measured GAA in 3,000 and then 10,000 newborn blood spots, using a second-tier test to remove false positives 150,151. A three-tier program in British Columbia screened 135,372 newborns with only three referred for genotyping 152. The first infants identified prospectively were reported from Utah and New York in 2021, both treated by two weeks of age and developing normally 153. An alternative product ion cut second-tier testing by 98% in New York 154. The evidence review behind adding GAMT deficiency to the US Recommended Uniform Screening Panel was published in 2023 118. No comparable newborn screen exists for CTD in this corpus, and the urine ratio's insensitivity in females is one obstacle.

Section 7

Creatine MR spectroscopy as a diagnostic tool

MRS is the only test that looks directly at brain creatine, which is the deficit all three disorders share. In each of the discovery papers it was the decisive observation 76,85,1. It remains part of every diagnostic algorithm in the corpus, alongside the body-fluid markers and genetic testing 155,156. A review in 2017 set out its uses: measuring creatine and phosphocreatine by proton MRS, estimating creatine kinase flux by phosphorus MRS, and following treatment 157.

The spectra carry more than a missing peak. In GAMT deficiency, GAA itself can be seen, first in single-voxel spectra 76,129 and then with chemical shift imaging, which showed GAA more prominent in gray than white matter 158. Spectroscopic imaging can quantify the deficit directly. In one family, total creatine was 1.6 mM in an affected boy, 4.6 mM in his heterozygous sister and 7.5 mM in controls 159. Muscle spectroscopy separates the disorders further. Phosphorus MRS showed no phosphocreatine in the muscle of an adult with GAMT deficiency 160, whereas muscle creatine was normal in a patient with CTD 89. This difference supports separate routes of creatine supply to brain and muscle 161.

MRS has two limits as a screen. In heterozygous females, brain creatine reductions are smaller and variable, and individual values can overlap with controls 101,162. As a general test in developmental delay, its yield is low in children without neurological signs: in one cohort of 109 children, MRI or MRS contributed to a diagnosis in 9% and MRS was diagnostic in one 163. A single-center review found 2 of 14 males referred for MRI with global developmental delay had brain creatine deficiency 164. MRS is therefore most useful when the clinical picture or the urine markers already point to a creatine disorder, and when a female carrier needs a functional read-out.

MRS is also the main biomarker of treatment. It documented the restoration of brain creatine in GAMT and AGAT deficiency within the first reports 76,78,85. Serial proton and phosphorus spectra showed that brain creatine recovers less completely in GAMT than in AGAT deficiency, and better once blood GAA is lowered 165. Fourteen years of quantitative follow-up in one GAMT patient showed brain creatine rising without reaching control ranges 166. In CTD, the absence of a rise on MRS has been the standard evidence that a treatment failed to reach the brain 1,167,168. Brain creatine on MRS is one of the seven outcomes in the 2025 core outcome set for trials in CTD and GAMT deficiency 169. A 2026 proposal calls for a harmonized pediatric MRS protocol, after a review found that the acquisitions in patients' radiology reports vary 170.

A newer route images creatine through chemical exchange saturation transfer (CrCEST). The fast exchange of creatine's amine protons makes it possible to image creatine with little contribution from phosphocreatine 171. CrCEST was first applied to skeletal muscle 172. GAMT knockout mice, which lack brain creatine, were then used to separate the creatine signal from protein signals in brain 173,174. At 7 T in human brain, the signal mixes creatine with other contributions 175. In 26 volunteers at 3 T, the guanidinium CEST signal correlated with MRS-measured total creatine at r = 0.66 176. These methods promise maps of creatine at high resolution, but none in this corpus has yet been applied to patients with a creatine deficiency.

Section 8

Why creatine loss harms the brain

The brain gets creatine from two sources, and the disorders show how the two interact. Creatine crosses the blood-brain barrier through SLC6A8 against a concentration gradient 177, but the entry is limited: after a large injection in rats, brain creatine rose only slightly 178. The brain also synthesizes creatine. AGAT, GAMT and SLC6A8 are expressed in neurons and glia throughout the rat brain 179, but fewer than 20% of cells co-express both synthetic enzymes, so GAA must often pass between cells to become creatine 180,181. The transporter appears to be absent from the astrocytes that line the barrier, which could explain why the brain depends on its own synthesis 179,180,182. This architecture explains the clinical split. Oral creatine reaches the brain through SLC6A8, which is intact in the synthesis defects. In CTD, local synthesis cannot compensate, because cells also need the transporter to take up creatine made by their neighbors.

GAMT deficiency adds a second mechanism, the accumulation of GAA. Guanidino compounds were known convulsants long before the disorders: γ-guanidinobutyric acid causes seizures 183, and guanidino compounds raised in uremia inhibit GABA and glycine responses 184. In 2002, GAA was shown to activate GABA-A receptors 185, and GAA is a potent GABA mimetic at cerebellar granule cells 186. But the evidence is mixed. Activation in cultured cerebellar granule cells required millimolar GAA 187, and GAA was not epileptogenic in neuronal networks or hippocampal slices at patient-relevant concentrations 188,189. In Gamt mutant mice, by contrast, systemic GAA affects GABA-A receptor function and seizure threshold 190. How much of the epilepsy in GAMT deficiency is due to GAA, rather than to creatine loss, is still open.

Animal models reproduce the deficit and many of its effects. GAMT-deficient mice showed high GAA and low creatine 191,192, with low body weight and impaired fertility 192. Ubiquitous Slc6a8 knockout mice lack brain and muscle creatine and show cognitive deficits 193,194. A brain-specific knockout was developed in 2012 195, and brain-specific knockouts reproduce the cognitive deficits with normal peripheral creatine 196,197. Deleting Slc6a8 on postnatal day 5 caused cognitive deficits, whereas deleting it on day 60 did not 198, which suggests a developmental window. A knock-in rat with the patient mutation Y389C adds a second species 199. CTD mice share EEG abnormalities with patients 200, and transcriptomics points to particular vulnerability of parvalbumin interneurons 201. Organoids from patients' stem cells reproduce neuronal deficits in a human system 202.

The models also show what creatine loss does not do. GAMT knockout mice run as far as controls and respond normally to myocardial infarction 203. Muscle dystrophy in AGAT knockout mice is reversed by creatine 204. Phosphorus MRS in patients with CTD found low phosphocreatine without raised lactate or lowered pH 205. These results suggest that tissues adapt to living without creatine better than the energy-buffer theory would predict, and that the brain's vulnerability may lie in development, in particular cell types, or in roles beyond energy.

Section 9

Treatment: from creatine supplements to the search for a CTD therapy

The synthesis defects. Treatment began with the discovery itself. Oral creatine raised brain creatine in the first GAMT patient 76 and normalized it over 25 months of treatment 78. Creatine alone did not control every patient's seizures, so treatment turned to lowering GAA. Restricting dietary arginine was tried in 1998 206. In 2001, arginine restriction plus ornithine supplementation, which inhibits AGAT 207, lowered GAA and nearly abolished seizures in a girl who had not responded to creatine alone 208. Sodium benzoate was in use by 2006 148, and a retrospective analysis linked lower glycine and higher ornithine to lower GAA 209. The added value of each component is uncertain. In one patient, ornithine and creatine lowered CSF GAA by 80%, while 18 further months of arginine restriction lowered it by only another 8% 210. In a mouse model, creatine itself lowered GAA by repressing AGAT 211, the same feedback first described in 1959 32.

The decisive result for the synthesis defects is that early treatment can prevent the disease. A neonate with GAMT deficiency treated from birth had normal development at 14 months 148, and a boy with AGAT deficiency treated from four months developed normally, unlike his two sisters 212. Long-term data now support this. AGAT-deficient patients treated from 4 and 16 months had normal development at 10 and 11 years 7, and in GAMT deficiency, younger siblings treated earlier had distinctly better outcomes, with the two treated as neonates indistinguishable from healthy controls 213. In GAMT deficiency diagnosed later, a registry of 22 patients reported improved severity scores in 13 and seizure freedom in 11, but normal development in only one 214. Even adults respond: a woman who began creatine at 52 became seizure-free 215. This evidence is the rationale for newborn screening. New approaches for GAMT deficiency include liver-directed gene therapy, which normalized plasma creatine and lowered GAA in mice 216, and CNS-directed gene therapy 217.

Creatine transporter deficiency. CTD has resisted treatment since its discovery, because oral creatine needs the missing transporter to reach the brain 1. The first strategy was to stimulate the brain's own synthesis with the precursors arginine and glycine. Results have been mixed. One boy improved on arginine with a rise in brain creatine 218, whereas four boys on arginine for nine months did not 219. Combined creatine, arginine and glycine produced no lasting brain creatine rise or cognitive gain in nine boys followed for four to six years 167, and helped only muscular symptoms in six severely affected patients 168. A systematic review of 28 cases found that 36% responded with a rise in brain creatine or clinical improvement 220. In an international registry, no male on combined therapy improved or deteriorated, while two of three females improved, which suggests the combination may halt progression in males 221. Several females have improved markedly on the combination 222,102,223, as has a boy with a hypomorphic variant, although his brain creatine did not rise 224. Glycine and arginine can raise homocysteine, which requires monitoring 225.

The second strategy is a creatine analog or prodrug that can cross membranes without the transporter. Creatine ethyl ester raised creatine uptake in patient fibroblasts but did not help four patients or raise their brain creatine 226. It degrades rapidly to creatinine 227. Cyclocreatine, a creatine analog, improved cognition in brain-specific knockout mice 195 and later improved cognitive, autistic and epileptic features in a mouse model 228. Its development has stalled on toxicity, however. Chronic dosing caused seizures and brain lesions in rats 229, and a dog study was terminated early because of severe toxicity 230. Cyclocreatine also enters cells mainly through SLC6A8 itself 231, which weakens the rationale for using it to bypass the transporter.

Dodecyl creatine ester (DCE) is the leading prodrug in the corpus. Among a series of creatine fatty esters, DCE raised creatine 20-fold in fibroblasts from patients with CTD 232. Packaged in lipid nanocapsules, it crossed an in vitro blood-brain barrier model 233. As an intranasal nanoemulsion, it improved object recognition and raised striatal ATP in Slc6a8 knockout mice 234. Studies in several species, including non-human primates, showed that intranasal DCE reaches neurons by the nose-to-brain pathway 235. Biomarkers of response have been proposed from proteomics, FDG-PET and DNA methylation in treated mice 236,237,238. No clinical efficacy data for DCE are in this corpus.

The third strategy restores the transporter. Many pathogenic variants misfold and are retained in the endoplasmic reticulum. The chemical chaperone 4-phenylbutyrate rescued several of these variants in cells 106, and drug-like correctors that bind SLC6A8 rescued creatine uptake for 10 to 20 percent of common missense variants and raised brain creatine in knock-in mice 239. Gene therapy is the most direct route. Intracisternal delivery of Slc6a8 in knock-in rats restored creatine in the hindbrain and prevented locomotor defects but did not correct forebrain phenotypes 240. Perinatal gene transfer in knockout mice prevented juvenile hypoconnectivity in functional MRI but left early cognitive deficits 241. A different idea bypasses the transporter by delivering the two synthetic enzymes, which raised creatine up to 11.6-fold in patient fibroblasts 242.

The field is now preparing for trials. A prospective natural history study provides the control data 97, a core outcome set defines what trials should measure 169, and a parent-led advocacy organization has supported registries, specimen repositories and newborn screening 243. A 2022 review summarized the state of treatment: AGAT and GAMT deficiency respond to high-dose oral creatine, and current treatments help about one third of patients with CTD 244.

Section 10

Open questions

Five questions stand out from this corpus. First, what part of the brain damage is due to the loss of creatine's energy-buffer function, and what part to its other roles or to GAA? The mouse models show surprising tolerance of creatine loss in muscle and heart, which sharpens the question for the brain. Second, is there a developmental window for CTD therapy? The neonatal-versus-adult deletion result in mice suggests there is, and gene therapy results so far correct some phenotypes but not others. Third, can CTD be found early? Newborn screening works for GAMT deficiency, but CTD has no established newborn screen, and the urine marker misses many females. Fourth, can MRS become a quantitative trial endpoint? That requires harmonized acquisition and quantification that does not depend on creatine as an internal reference. Fifth, which of the CTD candidates will reach patients? Cyclocreatine stalled on toxicity, DCE has animal and primate data but no published clinical results here, and correctors and gene therapy are preclinical. The history of the synthesis defects suggests that if a therapy reaches the brain early enough, outcomes can be transformed.

A note on sources

What this review could and could not see

This review was written from abstract-derived summaries, so it cannot report what papers said only in their methods, figures or discussion. About 60 papers in the corpus, most of them published before 1975, had no abstract available; their summaries restate only the title, and the review cites them only for what the title establishes. Abstracts compress results and can overstate them, and where two studies disagree, for example on the cardiac findings in CTD or on GAA toxicity, the review reports the disagreement rather than resolving it.

The bibliography is broad but not exhaustive. It was built by nine search lanes, a cross-citation pass and a forward-citation pass, and its numbers and dates are those in the summaries. Clinical trials registered but not yet published are outside its scope, so the status of human trials of DCE or gene therapy for CTD may have moved beyond what is described here.
